The Pigeons of Magnet.

A homing club in a Tasmanian mining town

Magnet may be remembered for its mine and tramway, but the town’s sporting interests extended well beyond the familiar games of cricket and football. For a time, homing pigeons were serious business too.

In November 1919, a meeting at Magnet established a homing society. Between fifteen and twenty enthusiasts enrolled, and F. Hogan was appointed secretary.1 From that modest beginning grew a club whose activities reached well beyond the town.

A race back to Magnet

Homing pigeon racing relied on a bird’s ability to return to its loft after being released elsewhere. At Magnet, reported release points included Ridgley, Burnie and Guildford Junction. Each race gave owners and supporters a reason to watch the skies and compare the performances of their birds.

By 1925, the homing society had become a substantial local organisation. Its annual meeting recorded 32 members, 20 races and 408 race entries. F. Barratt headed the flying-points competition with 45 points.2

Something for the children

The birds also brought younger residents into the excitement. A children’s race from Magnet Junction in June 1924 attracted 54 entries, with fifteen birds shown. Local donors supplied prizes, giving the event a place in the wider social life of the town.3

There were races in which women nominated birds as well. In July 1924, a Guildford Junction race for a silver teapot was won by a bird appropriately named Never Fail, nominated by Miss Cleaver.4

The hawks had other ideas

Racing was not without its setbacks. Hawks were a recurring problem. In the 1926 Burnie Derby, 50 entries had been recorded, but the number of birds available at liberation had fallen to seven following losses attributed to hawks.5

For the owners, every bird represented time spent raising, training and caring for it. A successful return was worth celebrating; a missing bird meant disappointment and, sometimes, the loss of a prized racer.

More than a sporting club

The Magnet homing enthusiasts were part of the town’s social life as well. Club gatherings, dances and fundraising events brought supporters together outside racing days. Newspaper reports from 1936 record renewed club activity, competitions from several north-western release points and social evenings supported by local families.6

It is an appealing little chapter in the history of Magnet: a remote mining settlement where a group of residents found sport, companionship and competition in the flight of homing pigeons. Beyond the mine workings and machinery, the races offer another glimpse of the community that grew around them.
