Drilling into the Depths.

Rock Drills and Underground Mining at Magnet, Tasmania

Beneath the forested hills of north-western Tasmania, the miners of Magnet worked to recover silver-lead and zinc ores from the rock. Each new drive and working face demanded a way through solid ground. Drilling was one of the essential tasks of underground mining, and the machinery used to carry it out became part of a much larger system of compressors, pipes, workshops and skilled labour.

A newspaper account from 1908 and the extraordinary detail of the mine’s 1940 clearance catalogue bring this part of Magnet’s story into focus. They take us from a proposed improvement in the mine’s development to the rock drills, drilling steels and supporting equipment offered for sale when the operation was dismantled.12

Breaking the rock

For generations, hard-rock miners drilled holes by hand, striking and turning a steel tool until it had penetrated far enough to receive an explosive charge. The work required strength, patience and experience. Hole by hole, the miners prepared the rock for blasting and removal.

Pneumatic percussion drills changed the pace and character of drilling. Powered by compressed air, a moving piston struck the drilling steel repeatedly while a mechanism turned it between blows. The machine concentrated a succession of impacts on the rock, allowing holes to be advanced mechanically. But it brought other demands: a dependable air supply, serviceable drilling steels, replacement parts and workshops capable of keeping the equipment in order.

A proposal in 1908

On 5 September 1908, the North Western Advocate and the Emu Bay Times reported comments by Magnet mine chairman W. F. Petterd following a visit by company directors. Petterd described recent improvements to the concentrating mill and said that the directors would recommend the installation of rock drills to assist development.2

The proposal came during a difficult year at Magnet, when a prolonged industrial dispute had affected the workforce and the town. In that setting, the intention to introduce rock drills was part of the company’s discussion of how work at the mine might be advanced.

The next clear picture of specific drilling equipment in the records examined here comes much later, in the 1940 clearance catalogue.

Nine pneumatic drills

Among the machinery offered at Magnet’s final auction were nine identifiable Ingersoll Rand rock drills. Seven were listed as J.A. 55 pneumatic jack hammers, while two were S.A.R. 85 pneumatic stope hammers. The catalogue identifies the machines individually, complete with their lot numbers.1

The J.A. 55 jack hammer

The J.A. 55 entries are particularly informative. Each of the seven machines was offered with a column, cradle and clamps. These fittings supported and positioned a drill while it worked into the rock, helping the operator control the direction of the hole and the force of the machine.

A period technical description identifies the J.A. 55 as a dry-type pneumatic rock drill. It worked by percussion, with a mechanism that rotated the steel between impacts. The equipment at Magnet was therefore more substantial than the word “jack hammer” might suggest to a modern reader: the catalogue describes complete mounted drilling assemblies rather than ordinary handheld demolition tools.4

The S.A.R. 85 stope hammer

The two S.A.R. 85 machines belonged to another class of pneumatic rock drill. A stope is an underground excavation from which ore is extracted, and stope hammers were developed for drilling in these working areas. Their separate listing alongside the J.A. 55 machines shows the range of drilling equipment held at Magnet in 1940.

The compressors behind the drills

A pneumatic drill depended on compressed air delivered from machinery elsewhere in the mine. Magnet’s catalogue records a large Ingersoll Rand twin-cylinder horizontal air compressor as Lot 127, together with a steel air receiver under Lot 129. Another compressor, made by George W. Kelly & Lewis, appeared as Lot 355.1

Lot 496 offered an Ingersoll Rand Model T40 two-stage compressor. The auction description called it almost new and stated that it had done very little work. Numerous lengths of air hose, along with fittings and other compressed-air equipment, were also included in the sale.

These were the less visible necessities of mechanical drilling. Air had to be generated, stored and carried to the working places. A drill at the rock face was the final link in an installation that extended back through hoses, pipes and machinery.

Steel, bits and the blacksmith’s shop

The No. 2 Blacksmith’s Shop contained an extensive assortment of drilling steel. Lots 250–263 describe sets of seven-eighths-inch hexagonal hollow steel in lengths from two to seven feet, with separate entries for telescopic steel and jack-hammer steel.1

The catalogue also records 37 reconditioned jack bits, 14 new jack bits and a further quantity of bits stored in the Engineers’ Shop. An Ingersoll Rand jack-bit sharpener, listed as Lot 75, came with a power feed, emery stone and associated drive equipment.

Every hole drilled against hard stone placed wear on the equipment. Bits required sharpening, steels needed attention and the machines depended on a supply of serviceable mechanical parts. Magnet’s auction lists spare rifle bars, pistons, chucks and oil-line bottles, as well as a four-gallon tin of Shell rock-drill oil.

Such entries give the workshops an important place in the story. The progress made underground relied on the men and machines responsible for keeping the drills ready for another shift.

Working with air, water and dust

Percussion drilling was demanding work. Its repeated impacts brought noise and vibration, while the cutting and breaking of rock produced dust. Across hard-rock mining, controlling airborne dust became an important part of protecting underground workers.

Magnet’s auction catalogue places numerous lengths of air and water hose alongside its pneumatic equipment, and the drilling steels in the blacksmith’s shop are described as hollow. The J.A. 55 itself is described in a technical manual as a dry-type drill. These details illustrate the variety of equipment associated with underground drilling, from air-driven machines to the hoses and steels that supported the wider operation.14

Whatever the particular arrangement at a working face, the physical demands on the miner were considerable. Operating and positioning drilling equipment formed part of the daily labour required to advance the mine.

Diamond drilling in 1936

Drilling was also important when Magnet’s future mineral prospects came under examination. The project’s research summary of the 1936 annual report records that mining work ceased on 1 October that year and that diamond drilling began to test for further lodes. Approximately twelve men were employed in this work.3

Diamond drilling differed from the percussion drilling used to prepare blast holes. Its rotating cutting equipment could recover samples of rock, giving those examining the ore body direct evidence of the ground beneath the surface. At Magnet it formed part of the effort to investigate the remaining mineral prospects.

The final inventory

By 1940, Magnet’s mining machinery, equipment and buildings were being offered for sale. The rock drills occupied only a small part of the long auction catalogue, yet their entries are among its most revealing technical records.

There were seven J.A. 55 jack hammers with their mounting equipment, two S.A.R. 85 stope hammers, compressors, air receivers, hoses, hollow drilling steels, sharpening machinery and spare parts. Together they form a remarkably detailed inventory of a hard-rock drilling installation at the end of the mine’s working life.1

At a distance of more than eighty years, those catalogue descriptions bring the underground operation into sharper focus. Behind the work of breaking and removing ore stood a network of mechanical power, tool maintenance and practical skill. The drills themselves were only one part of it, but their surviving descriptions help recover something of the machinery and labour that made mining at Magnet possible.
