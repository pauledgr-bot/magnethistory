Drilling into the Depths.

Rock Drills and Underground Mining at Magnet, Tasmania

Beneath the forested hills of north-western Tasmania, the miners of Magnet worked to recover silver-lead and zinc ores. Before the ore could be extracted, passages had to be driven, working faces opened and rock broken. Drilling was essential to that work.

Surviving records offer glimpses of how the task was approached. A newspaper report from 1908 records a proposal to install rock drills. More than three decades later, the mine’s 1940 clearance auction catalogue gives an unusually detailed inventory of pneumatic drills, compressors, steel and spare parts. Together these sources help reconstruct one part of Magnet’s underground operation, while leaving important questions unanswered.12

From hand drilling to machines

In nineteenth-century hard-rock mining, blast holes could be made by hand with a hammer and drilling steel. The steel was struck and turned repeatedly, gradually deepening the hole. It was demanding work, and progress depended on the ground, the tools and the miners’ skill.

Mechanical percussion drills delivered repeated blows through a steel tool, while a rotation mechanism turned it between strikes. Compressed-air machines required much more than the drill itself: a supply of air, hoses, steel, maintenance tools and, often, mounting equipment.

The exact date mechanical rock drilling began at Magnet has not been established. The early history should not be reconstructed from the much later equipment list alone.

The proposal of 1908

On 5 September 1908, the North Western Advocate and the Emu Bay Times reported comments made by the mine’s chairman, W. F. Petterd, after a visit by company directors. Petterd described improvements to the concentrating mill and said the directors would recommend installing rock drills to assist development.2

The statement was made during a period of industrial conflict at Magnet. It establishes that mechanised drilling was under consideration; it does not establish that machines were purchased, installed or put to work immediately afterwards. Those questions need further contemporary evidence.

The machines listed in 1940

The strongest evidence for identifiable underground rock drills comes from the 1940 clearance catalogue. It names two Ingersoll Rand models among the machinery offered for sale: the J.A. 55 pneumatic jack hammer and the S.A.R. 85 pneumatic stope hammer.1

The J.A. 55 jack hammer

Seven separate lots describe J.A. 55 machines supplied with columns, cradles and clamps. These accessories are important: they show that the drills could be supported and positioned while holes were made in rock. The auction description is specific enough to identify the model, but tells us nothing about where each machine was used, how old it was or whether it remained serviceable.1

A preserved quarrying-equipment manual describes the Ingersoll Rand J.A. 55 as a dry-type pneumatic rock drill. This is a technical description of the model, not a record of Magnet’s particular machines or their operating history.4

The S.A.R. 85 stope hammer

Lots 131 and 152 identify two S.A.R. 85 stope hammers. In mining, a stope is an excavation from which ore is extracted; stope hammers were a specialised class of rock drill intended for work in such spaces. The catalogue gives no dimensions, operating capacities or records of how these individual machines were deployed.1

It would be premature to assign these two drills exact weights, performance figures or positions underground without a contemporary S.A.R. 85 manual or another reliable technical source.

The air supply behind the drills

Pneumatic drilling depended on compressors and associated equipment. Magnet’s clearance catalogue lists a large Ingersoll Rand twin-cylinder horizontal air compressor (Lot 127), a Kelly & Lewis horizontal compressor (Lot 355), and an Ingersoll Rand Model T40 two-stage compressor (Lot 496). It also includes a steel air receiver (Lot 129), as well as lengths of air hose and fittings.1

The catalogue describes the T40 as almost new and having done very little work. That is the auction catalogue’s assessment, not independently verified evidence of its operating hours. Likewise, the record does not identify which compressor powered any particular drill underground.

Nevertheless, the collection shows that rock drilling was part of a larger mechanical installation, dependent on maintained machinery and an air supply beyond the working face.

Drilling steel, bits and workshop maintenance

The No. 2 Blacksmith’s Shop entries record sets of seven-eighths-inch hexagonal hollow drilling steels in lengths from two to seven feet. The catalogue distinguishes telescopic steels from jack-hammer steels in Lots 250–263. Other entries include 37 reconditioned jack bits (Lot 280), 14 new jack bits (Lot 294), and a quantity of additional bits in the Engineers’ Shop.1

Lot 75 describes an Ingersoll Rand jack-bit sharpener, with power feed, emery stone and drive fittings. Spare chucks, pistons, rifle bars and oil-line bottles appear in Lots 160–166. A tin of rock-drill oil is listed in Lot 731. These apparently small items are valuable evidence of the continual attention rock-drilling equipment required.

They also show why the underground work cannot be separated from the surface workshops. Drill steels wore, cutting edges needed attention, and machines required replacement components.

Dust, water and working conditions

Drilling hard rock could create noise, vibration and airborne mineral dust. In hard-rock mining generally, exposure to respirable dust, including silica where present, was a serious occupational hazard. Water-assisted drilling was among the measures developed to reduce dust at the source.

The Magnet catalogue includes numerous air and water hoses alongside the rock drills, and it separately lists hollow steels. Those items are consistent with a drilling installation that had provision for water, but they do not prove how every drill was operated. The J.A. 55 manual’s description of that model as dry-type is an important caution against treating adjacent water-hose lots as evidence of water-fed J.A. 55 machines.14

Nor do general historical risks establish that a particular Magnet miner contracted a drilling-related disease. Such a statement would require individual medical or other contemporary evidence.

Diamond drilling in 1936

A different kind of drilling appears near the end of Magnet’s operating history. The project’s Master Events Timeline, drawing on the 1936 annual report, records that mining ceased on 1 October 1936 and diamond drilling commenced to investigate possible new lodes. Approximately twelve men were employed in this work.3

Diamond drilling served an exploratory purpose distinct from drilling blast holes with pneumatic rock drills. A rotating cutting tool could penetrate the ground and recover rock samples for examination. The research summary does not identify Magnet’s diamond-drilling machine, and the original annual report remains the necessary source for checking the full account before book publication.

When the equipment went under the hammer

In 1940 the mine’s machinery was offered for disposal. The rock drills were only a small part of a much larger clearance, but the catalogue preserves details rarely available in general accounts of a mining town: model numbers, accessories, specialised steels, compressors and workshop tools.1

It is tempting to picture the individual machines in Magnet’s drives and stopes. Yet the surviving inventory does not reveal their operators, their precise locations, when they were acquired, or their condition at the time of sale. Nor does their inclusion establish that every machine found a buyer.

What can be said is that by 1940 Magnet possessed a substantial collection of pneumatic rock-drilling equipment, supported by compressors, drilling steels and facilities for servicing the tools. These records help recover a practical dimension of the mine’s history: the demanding work required to penetrate the rock, and the equipment behind the men who carried it out.

Questions still open

When were rock drills first installed at Magnet? When were the J.A. 55 and S.A.R. 85 models acquired? How were water and dust suppression managed underground? What make of diamond-drilling machine was used in 1936? These are questions for further research, not details to be filled in by assumption.
