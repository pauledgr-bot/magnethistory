Mine & machinery · 1934

When Water Ruled the Mine.

At Magnet, rain could decide whether the machinery kept running.

Water was one of the Magnet mine’s most persistent problems. Too little of it could bring operations to a standstill. The reports from 1934 tell the story particularly well: a mine waiting for rain in March, working again in April, and surrounded by overflowing dams by December.

In a wet part of Tasmania, it might seem extraordinary that a mine could be short of water. Yet water supplies were essential to the operation of the plant, and dry spells repeatedly tested the people trying to keep it going.

Back to work

Heavier rain lifted the dam level, and mining resumed late in the month after roughly four months of inactivity. Government assistance had helped retain about 40 to 42 men on pumping and maintenance during the stoppage.

June 1934

A timely rise

More rain raised the water level from 40 feet to 51 feet, preventing a further closure. Once again, rainfall had made the difference between continuing and stopping.

July 1934

Another narrow escape

Rainfall of 186 points helped prevent another shutdown. The water supply still demanded attention even in the middle of winter.

December 1934

Now the dams were overflowing

By December the picture had changed completely. Rainfall reached 530 points, the dams were overflowing, and the mine produced 50 tons of concentrates in a fortnight.

The machinery depended on it

Water affected the ability to operate, not just the amount of ore being won. Throughout 1934 the Magnet Prospecting Syndicate was also attending to equipment. An electric pump was installed at No. 16 level, while repairs were made to the large electric winding motor. Both were part of efforts to improve efficiency and reliability.

And 1934 was not an isolated example. The mine’s earlier reports tell much the same story, with water shortages interrupting production even as the district experienced heavy rainfall at other times.

Other years, the same problem

  • 1926: A late-April report recorded 669 points of rain in a week and the Arthur River dam full.
  • 1928: Ore production stopped in January amid water difficulties. Rain in April and full dams by early May allowed operations to move towards resumption.
  • 1936: The mine reopened on 27 April after a stoppage dating from Christmas. A report recorded 1,030 points of rain to the preceding Monday.

These little snapshots are a reminder that a mine depended on much more than the ore beneath the ground. At Magnet, the weather above ground could determine whether the engines ran, the men worked and concentrates were produced.
